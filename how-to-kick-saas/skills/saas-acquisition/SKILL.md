# SaaS Acquisition & Growth Marketing

This skill packages the "Acquisition & Growth" part of the open-source book *How to Kick SaaS* by Jason M. Long (JH-Media-Group/how-to-kick-saas). It captures the author's own process, numbers, tools, step sequences and lessons for getting paying users into a SaaS, exactly as he states them. It is not a general marketing textbook — everything below is attributed to the book.

## When to Use

- The user is starting a SaaS and asks where paying customers will come from, or how to get the first users.
- The user asks how to acquire the first 200–300 users, or how to close the first paying deals.
- The user asks about SEO, organic search, keyword research, content planning, article writing/formatting or landing pages.
- The user asks about Google Adwords/PPC, paid ads, ad testing, ad creative or negative keywords.
- The user asks about outbound/cold email campaigns, email titles, A/B testing subjects or lead lists.
- The user asks about social media marketing, retargeting, tracking pixels, Lookalike Audiences or GDPR for retargeting.
- The user asks about marketing automation, drip/nurture/onboarding emails, the customer buying cycle, lead scoring, tagging, triggers or CRM escalation.
- The user asks about lifetime deals (LTDs), LTD marketplaces, LTD refunds or LTD support load.
- The user asks about affiliates, partnerships, webinar-driven affiliate deals or commission/LTV splits.
- The user asks about readiness before a growth push: onboarding, upsells, personas, adaptive design or competitors.

## When NOT to Use

- The question is about pricing and appraisement, validation, or the advisory board process that precedes acquisition (the book treats those separately; this file only covers the acquisition/growth chapters).
- The question is about retention/renewal economics, churn, or LTV/CAC pricing math beyond how the book uses LTV ≥ 3× CAC in an outbound campaign example.
- The question is about product development, design, or engineering — outside this file's source range.
- The user wants generic marketing advice unconnected to the book; this skill intentionally stays inside the source files.

## Getting Your First Users

The book's process for the first users, after the advisory board, is four steps (`acquiring-your-first-users.md`):

1. **Friends, Coercion, & Testimonials.** By this point you have used the advisory board for feedback, sold the first units to advisory-board people and know people will pay. Now collect testimonials from industry people — they do not need to use the system, only go on record saying it is exciting; the higher up and better known, the better. Reach out to people in your network or associated with the industry, take them to lunch, ask for feedback, then ask permission to quote them. Put the testimonial on your website with name, title, company and photo (the book gives fill-in-the-blank example quotes).
2. **Ask for referrals.** At that same meeting, ask who else might be interested. The key is to ask for names of people who can give advice, not to sell — unless the meeting is going very well and the lead is already naming people.
3. **Close some deals.** If the system is easy to use, easy to sign up for and useful, and the person in front of you is interested, just ask for the deal. The book scripts it: reference the referral's testimonial, ask "Want to get started with the system now?", then pull up the signup page, enter their info and ask for the credit card. If the answer is no, reassess the product and appraisement.
4. **Rinse, Repeat, & Reassess.** Plan on getting a few paying users, checking in regularly, asking lots of questions and changing the system. For systems the author has worked on, this process lasts "anywhere from a few weeks to more than a year"; the larger and more complex the SaaS, the more iterations needed. The goal is not perfection — "just good enough to get money coming in while keeping people mostly happy."

The chapter intro (`acquisition-getting-saas-users.md`) frames the scale: the typical founder does the math and realises "if you just had 200 or 300 users you'd be doing fine." It also states the core premise: "when you run a SaaS business, you are running a sales and marketing company," and since a SaaS scales over the internet, you will be doing digital marketing.

The book lists digital marketing for SaaS as falling into these categories (each a chapter): Organic Search Marketing, Marketing Automation, SaaS Lifetime Deals, Community Building, Affiliate Marketing, Outbound Campaigns, Conversion Rate Optimization, Social Media Marketing, and Pay Per Click Advertising. Community Building is defined as growing online communities with a common issue, through active and non-invasive communication (`saas-traction-lessons.md` adds that your community is made of people who want your project to work, so they tell you what they like).

## Organic Search & Content Marketing

**Position in the plan.** Organic search is placed first among the growth methods because it is one of the most effective and because it takes the longest, so it must start early (`organic-search-marketing/README.md`). Even if all research, planning and writing are done up front and articles are live on day one, it takes several months to rank. The reason given is the Google Sandbox: new sites are not trusted, and earning trust "takes somewhere between 3 and 6 months" (better technical SEO shortens the wait). Once you rank top for one keyword you often rank top for several others, and the cost of maintaining an organic position is often substantially less than paying for advertising — but you will still pay in the time to produce and distribute content.

**Author's proof story.** The team did research and planning, then wrote "about a dozen articles of around 1,000–1,200 words a piece" and posted them all at once on a brand-new blog. Six months later the phone started ringing and the contact form had been filling up (it was not notifying them — the book warns to test the contact form regularly). They had gone from zero to number one for at least five of the target keywords in six months, and "never spent a dime on ads."

**Content marketing is an investment.** Paid ads are like ordering take-out: easy, fast, but you cannot reasonably rely on it alone. Content/SEO is like buying an oven and refrigerator — expensive up front, cheaper long-term. The author says that, apart from a few enterprise systems, he has never met a successful SaaS owner who skipped or half-assed this step.

### Step 1 — Keyword research (about one day of work)

Keyword research is "the process of figuring out what keywords you should, can, and cannot rank for."

1. **Search volume.** Use Google's Keyword Planner. If you already spend on PPC you get exact numbers; otherwise you get a range. Google is the most reliable source; other tools give volume too. Ignore most of the PPC-related extras for now.
2. **Negative keywords.** Entering your best guesses yields about 1,500 suggestions, some wildly off. The author's example: searching doctor reviews returned dozens of headphone-review terms — the chain was Doctors → Dr. Dre → Beats by Dre → headphone reviews. He eliminated "Dre" and "headphones" as negative keywords. Negative keywords matter even more for PPC because you pay for every off-target click.
3. **Filters.** Filter by who actually matters to your SaaS (e.g. only US searches, only the past six months).
4. **Pruning.** Export the list and delete anything off base, asking "who would be searching for this term." Example: "How to leave a Yelp review for a doctor" is a patient search, not a doctor search. Weed out the 1,000+ excess, but keep adding your own terms — self-added terms are often the most targeted and valuable. CTRL+F is your friend.
5. **Keyword difficulty.** The most important data point: it tells you whether a term is worth pursuing. Difficulty is scored 0–100, low = easier. Use Moz (`moz.com/explorer`) — a few free searches a day, or an account (free for 30 days) to run lists; drop the data into a spreadsheet and match volume to difficulty with a "Vlookup" formula.
6. **Assess value.** If new to SEO, go after the lowest-difficulty keywords first — ideally difficulty under 20. If that is impossible, take keywords in the low 20s "and just work your ass off." Highlight favourites, or colour-code ranges with conditional formatting.
7. **Keyword groups.** With a few hundred keywords left, sort them into topic groups. The dance-class example groups by style (Latin, Ballroom, Tap, Line) or by class type (Intro, Series, Kids, Private lessons), or by location — one spreadsheet tab per group.
8. **Domain authority.** The strength of your domain, earned over time. Rule of thumb: it is a waste of time to try to rank for keywords with difficulty above 20 if your domain authority is below 20; raise domain authority first. Check it on Moz or other systems.
9. **Long-tail keywords.** More specific phrases or complete thoughts, often spoken to Alexa/Google/Siri. Lower volume, may not even register in Keyword Planner, but long-tail keywords "account for 70% of all searches" and are far more targeted; "15–20% of all searches have never been searched before." Examples: "Where can I take a 6 week waltz class in Nashville?" — these are people ready to convert.
10. **SERP analysis.** SERP = Search Engine Results Page. Assess the current top results: plug long-tail keywords into Moz Keyword Explorer to see the top sites and their domain authority, then look at each top page and judge whether the content is specific, lengthy and well written. If it is not, you may stand a chance (the author often sees pages ranking for keywords not even on the page). Rate each SERP yourself, usually 1–5.

**Using the spreadsheet.** Two ways, both used: (a) sprinkle short-tail keywords around the homepage and site content — e.g. a tagline moving from "Register for dance classes" (difficulty 52, unworkable) to "sign up for dance classes online" (difficulty 33); (b) keyword your blog, which needs a plan (Step 2).

### Step 2 — Content planning

- **Retrofitting.** If articles already exist, go back and sprinkle keywords in where sensible, but check the difficulty of the terms you are replacing so you do not accidentally remove something that was helping.
- **Content spreadsheet.** One article per row, with columns for title, keywords, links and other items.
- **Two strategies.** (a) Many small articles, around 1,500 words each — good for varied keyword topics, multiple personas, or limited time; the book says this suits most people starting out. (b) The **Skyscraper Method** — one mammoth article of 10,000 words or more, good for SaaS with research time and a single focused topic. Tips for the skyscraper route: plan a backlink campaign, write smaller supporting articles and link them to the main one, promote the main article everywhere, and research the skyscraper SEO strategy.
- **Keywords per article.** Pick 3 keywords per article idea — all high value (low difficulty) and directly related to the article's main idea, in priority order (highest value/most related first).
- **Titles.** Titles come after keywords because the main keyword must be in the title, and the title must be catchy. Search Google for what others use, consider location-specific competition, then draft (the book's example: "How To Find Wedding Dance Lessons For A First Dance You'll Never Forget" — keyword early but may be truncated — versus "An Insider's Guide to Choosing the Best Wedding Dance Lessons" — catchier). Keywords get the article seen; the title earns the click.
- **LSI keywords.** Latent Search Index keywords appear at the bottom of page 1 as "Searches related to…". Pick 2–3 LSI keywords per article, e.g. via LSI Graph (`lsigraph.com`), and add them to the spreadsheet.
- **Resources.** Add reference material for the writer (even if it is you) and always note the current top site ranking for the main keyword so you know what to beat; add competitors' good articles so you miss no talking points.
- **Links.** Any good article needs two link types and should aim for a roughly 50/50 balance. Optimized links use anchor text that is the same or very close to one of your keywords; non-optimized links use other anchor text ("learn more in this article", "take the dance style quiz"). Internal links are great, external are fine too. If linking out, make sure a competitor did not write the target. The most important factor: the linked page must clearly relate to its anchor text and both must be relevant to the article.
- **Cornerstone content pyramid.** The content plan should resemble a pyramid: home page at the top, cornerstone content below, then major articles, then long-tail content at the bottom. Internal links should follow the pyramid — bottom upward and top downward. Cornerstone content = your most in-depth articles on your most essential topics.
- **Order and schedule.** Decide the posting order (does one article build on another?) and commit to a realistic schedule (one a week, one a month, or writing ten before launch). A 3-a-month pace followed by a 6-month hiatus kills a returning reader base.

### Step 3 — Writing and formatting

- **Do not cheap out.** The biggest mistake is hiring an overseas writer at "$4.75 an hour" to produce a 1,500-word article for $20; if the content is not worth reading, no one reads it and the bounce rate destroys the value of the ranking. You may hire a writer, but even the best needs training on your goals, branding, ideas and content plan. Content work is not just writing — it is research, outlining, editing, then formatting, designing, testing and analytics. A front-end developer/designer on the team helps; otherwise a contractor with HTML experience will do.
- **Outlining and research.** Do not skip it. Study competitors' articles on the topic. The major sections of the article should be about your three keywords, with an honourable mention for each LSI keyword.
- **Templates.** Start with free SEO/blog templates if new; write your own from successful past articles if experienced.
- **Keyword placement and structure.** Keyword 1 goes in the first `<h1>` and in the paragraph beneath it; continue for all three. Additional nested headers must be smaller than the three keyword headers. Then: put the main keyword in several places; put links in paragraphs focused on their topic; keep paragraphs short; use bold/italics/lists where natural; include an introduction and a summary that both contain the main keyword; add a CTA after the introduction and after the summary; highlight each keyword and link for later.
- **CTAs.** CTAs move readers from the blog to the page where they take the next step. Some SaaS make CTAs stand out; others blend them in to look less salesy — only you or A/B testing knows. Example CTA: "Looking for convenient and affordable dance classes near you? Click here to see how Dance Today helps you compare dance classes anywhere in the US for free!"
- **The right way to write.** The one thing that makes people read, share and remember the article is how much actionable information it gives to help solve the reader's problem. Do not write to a word count — do not look at the word count at all; fluff gets no readers. You are the expert and have done the research, so "don't hold back information" — with the exception of genuinely private material, share it; people will find it anyway, so let them find it with your article and bookmark or link to it.
- **Formatting to your CMS.** Copy-pasting the whole article rarely carries formatting over. Whether visual editor or HTML, check the rendering as you go.
- **Images.** Images make posts more interesting, help SEO and reduce bounce. Do not steal images; pay for Adobe Stock or Envato, or use a free provider like Pixabay. Tips: always rename images and add Alt text aligned with your keywords; resize photos before uploading so they do not affect loading; have a designer create your featured images.
- **Metadata.** The meta description is the preview text under the title in search results. Keep it attention-grabbing, easy to read, unique, no all caps, no incomplete sentences, no pile of exclamation points. Use the main keyword, keep it around 160 characters, and throw in an attention-grabbing statistic when possible (example given: "76% of couples never learn to move to music together…").
- **Pop-ups.** The author says they are annoying but they work and are the best way to coax readers to your site or onto your email list with valuable downloadable content. He uses Sumo; he has had a high click rate with a sticky top bar and a slide-in sidebar, while other SaaS owners find the full-screen "doormat" style converts best. A/B test style, timing, imagery, wording and landing page.
- **Recommended reading.** `moz.com/learn/seo` and the SEMrush academy courses.

## Paid Acquisition: PPC & Social

### Google Adwords (PPC)

The book covers Google Adwords specifically and says the lessons transfer to other paid ad systems. The framing story: on a blueberry you-pick-em farm, a **$200 Adwords budget brought in something like a thousand visitors** — it worked because of awareness (everyone knew what a you-pick-em farm was), active summer demand, regional competition only, almost no competing advertisers, a daily-updated blog and luck. When the author applied the same confidence to his first SaaS (BrainLeaf) with a budget of "probably something like 5k or 10k," it was all lost, because: people did not understand why they needed the product, nobody was searching for it, the market was country-wide/global, developers-and-marketers SaaS competitors all use PPC, the content was unfocused/self-promotional, and neither luck nor preparation was present.

The seven lessons:

1. **Test your keywords and cut the fat before scaling.** Do keyword research; test keywords with small campaigns first; weed out keywords, messaging or demographics that are not converting; only then scale.
2. **PPC is half about who is clicking and half about who isn't.** Most clicks came from people with no reason to click, wasting money. Spend time in the **exclude demographics** area for each item, or you are "flushing your money down the drain."
3. **Focus on quality content and CTR.** Great content gave a low bounce rate on the farm; the SaaS launch had only "we're launching yet another SaaS" articles with no social proof and a barely working product, so bounce was high and CTR abysmal. **A high CTR lowers your cost per click (CPC)**; with a poor CTR Google either does not show your ads or prices you out. CTR is also far easier to measure than waiting for conversions.
4. **Keywords should take users to pages with corresponding content.** Users searched, clicked, and landed on pages unrelated to the keyword. Keywords were also only tangentially related to the product, and the author had no concept of an "awareness ladder" at the time. Landing pages must also be optimized for conversion — the pages used were good content pages but were never built to convert.
5. **Stay on top of your progress.** He set up traffic analytics but **no conversion tracking in Google Analytics**, and only reviewed analytics weeks later when sales had not moved.
6. **Organic search conversion rates are not the same as paid rates.** He had wrongly treated his organic conversion rate as a constant for other channels; the difference, he concluded, was page content quality and awareness.
7. **What he should have done differently.** Understand the different levels of awareness for the product, then build content and ads matching users' awareness levels — essentially redoing the whole campaign.

### Social media ads

**Strategy.** The mistake is using one channel only; use everything together and reinforce with social media. A single Facebook ad alone is like walking into a bar and asking a random person out — you might get lucky, but probably not. Social media is for **building a brand** (customers trust you) and **filling the funnel**; use distracted attention to pique interest, remind people the product exists, and offer something useful. The book is blunt that you probably will not sell anything significant on social media because attention quality is poor — each post gets a few seconds of distracted scanning — but ads and interactions work well for awareness and nudging people into the funnel. Useful content: publishing blog articles, providing technical support, announcing launches; SaaS-specific advantages: talking about releases/updates and pointing to a free tier or free trial.

**Ad tricks (from BrainLeaf).**

- Use **pictures of your UI** in the ad creative — a modern UI increases curiosity versus the software prospects use today.
- **Take advantage of customers' social posts** about your product: get their message to your followers; if the profile fits, they are good case-study candidates.
- **Provide support via social media** if sustainable — good customer experience, customers are likely to share your profile, and the answers eventually start ranking in Google.

**Retargeting.** Called the "bread and butter of SaaS marketing": showing social ads to people who visited your website before. It is powerful for two reasons: (1) the list already showed intent, so more will click; (2) repeated ads build familiarity (reminding them of the webinar in your blog, or the free trial that would solve a work task). It is not technically cheaper per impression — placement still competes and the price per impression is the same — but the cost per conversion drops dramatically versus cold traffic: **if you pay $20 per conversion on regular campaigns, retargeting can generate conversions at $2 or $3.**

How it works: set up a **tracking pixel** the platform provides. The Facebook pixel fires a request to Facebook whenever a Facebook user visits your site; with almost 2 billion Facebook users there is a high chance the visitor is on Facebook and gets added to a list for later campaigns. The Twitter website tag and LinkedIn Insight Tag work the same way. The list can also generate **Lookalike Audiences** (e.g. Facebook's). **GDPR:** retargeting is generally OK provided you have a cookie disclaimer allowing visitors to decline cookies, and you are not building lists without explicit authorization (e.g. scraping emails, copying emails from business cards). You can also upload customer email lists — all major platforms match them to social profiles — and segment them (free-trial-but-not-converted; pushing to a higher tier; visitors who have not signed up). **Multi-channel retargeting** is fine and a good idea — multiple platforms' pixels on your site let you show the same ads to the same people across many sites — but going overboard (say 20+) can slow your landing page.

**Testing social ads.** On social, ads are cheap to show (versus TV or newspaper) and can include images, links and video, not just Adwords-style text. Every ad campaign consists of three parts: **the creative** (image, ad text, video), **the targeting** (how you define the audience) and **the offer** (what you ask people to do — sign up for email, buy at full price, get 50% off). Because variables are many and cost is low, social is perfect for testing: e.g. start with **5 ad headlines × 5 images**, build every combination, and see what wins — **a lifetime budget of $100 per variation and a few days** is generally enough to show the winner. Then vary the offer, and re-test creatives if you change the audience.

**The structure of the offer (the book's two funnel math examples).**

- *Free-trial offer:* a free-trial offer converts at 0.05%, so 10,000 impressions yield 5 trial sign-ups; at $1,000 spend that is **$200 per trial sign-up**. If trials convert to paid at 3% and average LTV is $800, then `($200 / trial sign-up) / (3% trial→paid rate) = $6,667 per paid sign-up` — paying $6,667 for a customer worth $800 is not viable.
- *Newsletter offer:* offering a PDF ebook download plus newsletter sign-up is a smaller ask with a compelling offer, so conversion rises from 0.05% to 2% — 200 sign-ups from 10,000 impressions for the same $1,000. If newsletter→free-trial conversion is 10%, 200 becomes 20 trials; the email sequence teaches product use so trial→paid jumps to 10%, giving 2 paid users at $800 LTV = $1,600 revenue for $1,000 spend, keeping $600 profit. Changing the offer and adding a funnel step dramatically improves the math.
- *Webinars as the offer:* webinars educate prospects at scale ("like answering everyone's questions on a video call, but with a hundred people at a time"), are lower-commitment than a call, and are **recordable** — a recording is valuable enough to trade an email address for and can be reused across hundreds of ads on all platforms. For cold audiences, a webinar is a hard first ask; better to drive them to your website or a relevant PDF ebook first, then "upsell" them to a webinar registration.

## Outbound Campaigns

**Definition.** An email/outreach campaign sent to people you may or may not have spoken to. Many treat it as trolling for fish at scale, but for SaaS sales that approach is "more likely to drive away users than pull them in."

**Legal note (stated twice and emphatically).** In the US you can send unsolicited emails to a business address if you follow the **CAN-SPAM Act** rules. In many other countries — the author names **Germany and Canada** — unsolicited email is illegal and can bring fines and worse. Check your region's laws before applying these principles.

**How not to do it.** The book quotes the classic bad cold email ("I clearly want to sell you this thing… PS. I am going to email you 4 or 5 more times till you definitely mark this as spam"). The fix: do your homework, send something genuine to people who you know have the problem you solve, and **do not send a thousand at a time — send ten or twenty.**

**What makes the author open an unsolicited email:** he knew someone associated with the sender; he knew the brand; the product related to something he used regularly; the email offered education he needed at that moment; something in it was funny or interesting; he was connected to the writer or they knew his friends/clients/peers. He actually read and responded when he needed something in it, or actually knew the sender.

**The model email.** The book dissects a highly personal one: subject "Richard from [some business you know] recommended I give you a call," then a message naming the referrer, the business, a specific problem the recipient has right now, and an in-person invite (coffee at the coffee shop beside your house on Tuesday or Wednesday, or after 3pm). It converts extremely well even when automated, but it requires early mornings and a donut expense account, and screwing it up kills credibility — so you cannot send a thousand.

**Anatomy of a good outbound email.** (a) **Personalization** — reference a real person who believes in you; if the referrer did not actually recommend you, do not add this part, because "starting off on a lie is the easiest way to trash any relationship." (b) **A reference** — a business the recipient definitely knows. (c) **The name your lead calls their own business** — the "business shortname," not what is online or in a list; it makes the recipient think you clearly know them. (d) **The thing you need help with** — state the problem you know they have, not a vague "are you struggling with whatever?" (e) **Locality** — an in-person location, which can be dropped if needed. (f) **A personal invite** — even if the meeting cannot happen, propose another kind (e.g. a teleconference, pastries delivered by Uber). The rule: the more of these items, the better. Take out what you do not have, and if you cannot take the time to research the list and are not actually doing sales, **do not send the email.**

**The economics.** This is a method of direct sales — automated, but you get on the phone, so it costs cash. Apply the appraisement rule: **LTV must be at least 3× CAC.** Worked example: at $100 per call (donuts and coffee included) and a close every three calls, it costs about $300 per sale. If the average client pays $25/month for one year, you will not make it; if the average customer pays $100/month and stays two years, that is $2,400 and "you just made a cool $2,100 from that $300 you spent."

**Testing your messaging.** "Probably the most important thing you can do" — a few words make a huge difference. The author has seen **an open-rate difference of as much as 27%** between the titles "Are you taking more referrals" and "I want to send you some new patients." Any email system worth using has A/B testing; if yours does not, get a new system. In every campaign they test with small word changes, send a few titles/messages to a small sample first, then grow.

**The title that always works.** "I want to send you more [FILL IN THE BLANK WITH THE THING YOUR CLIENT NEEDS]." For medical practices: "I want to send you more patients." Simple, to the point, fast to read. The message itself: keep it simple, make it fast, show off your accolades, do not be afraid to say you are selling something, and never lie (e.g. "We did this thing for someone else and they made a ton of money from it and we can do it for you as well" — then the conversation is only about budget and time).

**The standard 4-email outbound campaign:**

| Email | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Email 1 | Ask for a meeting — very short, clear you want a meeting and know their needs. |
| Email 2 | Check in to see if they got the first email — essentially a check-in that they are still paying attention. |
| Email 3 | The Case Study — can be longer; use one of your own projects, or a competitor/peer's case study if you have none; showing positive results makes a big difference. |
| Email 4 | The Breakup — make clear you are not contacting them again in this campaign; **almost 50% of contacts back come from this last email.** |

The book says to play with this setup — different personas respond to different messages and the standard 4-email process does not fit everyone.

**Sending rules (all stated as important).**

1. **Don't use your main domain.** Use something close, e.g. `getyourdomain.com` or `yourdomain.net`, so a spam report does not hurt the main domain.
2. **Authenticate your sending domain** — set up **DKIM and SPF**, or you have a higher chance of being blacklisted.
3. **Don't send links or images** in the first two emails. Put the full URL as plain text ("Check out our website at www.jhmediagroup.com") and let their system create the link; never attach files in those first emails — spam filters catch that in about a millisecond.
4. **Give Google some time to think about it** (like the Google Sandbox). If using **Quickmail, Woodpecker** or similar tools that send through Gmail, set up a Gsuite account and pay at least the **$5 per month**, then let it sit for a full **30 days** — send only a few emails to friends or coworkers; do not use it for business for at least 30 days.
5. **The tools of the trade.** The author likes **Quickmail.io**; **Woodpecker.co** has grown on him (better UX, Quickmail more functional; they are about the same) — try both.

Final note: sending a ton of emails can be powerful but really annoying — do it right, follow the rules, use it for good, and remember that with power comes responsibility.

## Marketing Automation

**Definition (chapter intro).** Marketing Automation is "the strategic automation of repetitive tasks in digital communications to produce new leads or upsells from existing users from platforms and systems including nurture emails, social media posts, direct emails, and even texts." Automated marketing typically includes teaching users how to use the system, educating them on growing their businesses (with or without the system), educating the user base on industry trends, improving engagement with the company, and supporting users by answering questions and responding to issues. Because SaaS is scalable, if you do not have a way to automate **Reach, Engage, Educate, Converse** regularly, it will not get done and you lose to businesses that can. The book calls marketing automation "absolutely necessary" for almost all SaaS businesses and says the rabbit hole goes deep.

**What it is NOT (three misconceptions):** it is not just email marketing or a fancy way to spam; it is not "set it and forget it"; it is not a magic bullet. Feature areas listed: Campaign Management, Email Marketing, Landing Pages, Lead Scoring, Lead Management, CRM Systems Integration, Social Media Marketing, Analytics. Marketing automation is **iterative** — constantly hypothesize, test and retest; no system does that for you.

**The conversion effect (the book's real case study).** A "where you start" baseline of essentially knowing your users, sending a welcome set and following up every other week gives an **initial conversion rate of 3%–5%**. A fully personalised flow — a user stumbles on a (deliberately placed) blog/social post, the messaging meets their needs, the homepage shows someone exactly in their role, the page messaging gets a trial started, onboarding focuses on their issues, onboarding emails match their role, a rep calls with questions that clearly fit them, they pay, and then they get ongoing role/industry education with matching graphics — produced a **new conversion rate of 23%** (described as a real case study that actually happened), and the book notes some companies have reached **50%**.

**The customer journey (6 stages).** A customer goes from awareness to advocacy: **1. Unawareness** (do they know they have a problem? if not, why not — a critical question); **2. Awareness** (they know the problem and are starting to look for solutions); **3. Consideration** (they find your solution among many, educate themselves, read reviews); **4. Purchase**; **5. Retention** (as written in the file); **6. Advocacy** (you nurtured them, they become an advocate — your most powerful sales tool). You must understand where someone is in this journey, and who they are, before you can send the right thing. The book's mental game: someone gives their email for an e-book, you send educational emails with triggers that reveal which feature set they care about and slowly move them into a persona; later an avid user gets tips, templates, improvement calls and a conference invite with persona-specific designs — they probably stay loyal; flip the communications and it makes no sense.

The Prague story (`storytime-learning-about-marketing-automation-the-hard-way.md`) is where the author learned the cycle the hard way: his marketing targets did not know they had the problem, so his pool of potential buyers was far smaller than he thought. The advice he received was to market to the right people or build an awareness system, check Facebook private groups testing solutions, and start a podcast or audience around the issue — "what I think you need is an audience before the product on this one."

**Lifecycle flow and contact campaigns.** Lead scoring does not relate only to emails; the book lists the SaaS customer contact campaigns that should be in place: **welcome, onboarding, nurture, product engagement, upsell and renewal** email campaigns, plus (beyond email) chatbot messages for different user types/flows, different kinds of phone calls, direct mail, and actually visiting customers. The user lifecycle flow it scores against is: **Visitor** (visited the website), **Prospect** (downloaded a lead magnet), **Activated User** (signed up), **Customer** (paid), **Active Customer** (paid and using), **Loyal Customer** (paid and increasing use).

What each stage receives (as the book lists it):

- **Visitor:** educational information about their problem; a request to sign up or give more information so we can qualify them.
- **Prospect:** product information, case studies, demos, sales presentation.
- **Activated User:** user onboarding, tips and best practices, offers for help, regular newsletter, product updates, educational content, requests to become a paying customer, unfinished flows.
- **Customer:** all activated-user items except the request to become a paying customer, plus customer onboarding.
- **Active Customer:** all activated-user items except the paying request, plus upsell campaign, notify user of usage limits, feature benefits, new releases, promotions, renewal campaign.
- **Loyal Customer:** all activated-user items except the paying request, plus partnership campaign, personal relationship/word-of-mouth building campaigns.

Other contact methods listed: chatbot messages when the user views the marketing site or accesses the SaaS, social media messages or posts, direct mail, an actual phone call, and a follow-up phone call. Protip: plan plenty of time for these emails — each needs design, copywriting and some development, they always take longer than expected, and you will iterate on them as soon as they start going out.

### Lead scoring, tagging and triggers (the concrete system)

**Definition.** "Lead scoring helps you identify who you need to be talking to and what information they need to receive in an automated fashion." It is based on two main things: **how much the system solves the user's problems, and how much the user fits the profile you are selling to.** Rather than a complex formula, the author thinks of it as an action- or task-focused process answering "at what point do I need to send what to who?" — assign values and correlate them to actions.

**Scoring attributes (the BrainLeaf system).** The first things you can almost always get are profile fields: **Name, Email, Role, Company Size, Business Type.** Name and email are required to process a user but carry no points. Then actions. The initial BrainLeaf points system:

- **How they got here:** 10 points — referral from an ad; 5 points — arrived via the top blog article on how to scope a project and read it for more than 5 minutes; 1 point — everything else.
- **Business type:** 1 point — Freelancer (they like to know); 5 points — Marketing (digital or traditional); 10 points — Development; Enterprise — business is escalated.
- **Business size (via a 3rd-party system):** 1 point — 1 person; 5 points — 2–10; 10 points — 11–25; 15 points — 25–100; more than 100 — sent to the sales team for follow-up every time.
- **Position in the business:** 1 point — Freelancer; 2 points — Founder; 2 points — CEO (often a freelancer, so scored lower); 5 points — Project Manager; 5 points — Marketing Manager; 10 points — CMO; 15 points — CTO.
- **Business name:** no points, but very large companies are sometimes escalated straight to sales.
- **System use:** started a new project — 5 points; more than one new project — 5 points; used a small business template — 5 points, a complex/development template — 10 points; added teammates — 1 point per teammate; sent a contract to a customer — 10 points.

BrainLeaf's target personas exclude freelancers. The three worked scenarios total **20, 46 and 58 points** respectively: scenario 1 (blog → freelancer → 1 person → Founder → new project → small template) is "definitely a freelancer" and should be lower on the contact list; scenario 3 (blog → Development → 33 people → Project Manager → new project → complex template → sent a contract) is a mid-sized development company and should be phoned as soon as possible. Sales works the list top-down by score. Automations built on top: any user scoring **over 60** triggers a CRM action to call or send a personal email; users scoring **30 or below** get automated emails explaining why they need the system (lower-level users often sit in the first couple of buying-cycle stages). The protip: do not overcomplicate — add the top indicators and actions first, add more later; a good lead scoring system makes a big difference to how fast and well you process users and make sales.

**Recommended systems.** Drip.com, Convertkit.com, Activecampaign.com and Marketo.com are listed as systems for setting up automated emails, managing lists, automations and triggers. **Not recommended for new SaaS:** Infusionsoft.com (effective but often too expensive to start) and Mailchimp.com (too simple for SaaS marketing automation — aside from Mandrill for transactional email). The author's own stack: Drip.com for email automation, paired with Pipedrive and Zapier "zaps," all integrated into Slack. Further reading the book points to: Myk Pono's Medium article on lead scoring and *The Definitive Guide to Lead Scoring* by Marketo.

## Lifetime Deals

**Definition.** A Lifetime Deal (LTD) is an introductory offer where the customer pays up front for a perpetual licence — usually unlimited access to the SaaS for a one-time flat fee. The book says LTDs are most often used to get the system started and gain initial users, and the author ran one with his product **BrainLeaf**. Key warning: **"LTDs are not an A-Series funding round"** — a LTD must be done only when the product is ready for market, because most LTD marketplaces have a no-questions-asked refund policy, so an unready product means refunds wipe out the work. "Remember that little problems become big problems when you scale your business" — fix the little things and make the system and support solid before starting.

The 12 lessons (as numbered in the file):

1. **Enable users to buy as many licenses as they want.** Users must be able to pay first, for as many licences as they want, on the payment landing page — some systems force a signup-then-pay flow that will not work. Tell people how many licences they can buy.
2. **Be very specific about the rules.** Have a page covering: how many licences can I buy; can I resell licences; what exactly does this cover; if I upgrade will this still apply; if you change the system do I keep the same deal; how many [value metric] does this cover. Without it you answer these questions about a hundred times a day during the deal.
3. **There is no rush, even if you think there is a rush.** If you need quick cash, a LTD is the wrong choice — raise money another way. There are always groups looking for a new LTD; if none will promote yours, your system may be too niche, not useful, or broken. ("LTDs" are sometimes called "Likely to Disappear.")
4. **Only certain SaaS systems can do LTDs.** Systems in niche markets whose target audience is unaware of LTDs will not work on the LTD marketplaces.
5. **Don't plan to do anything but focus on this sale while it runs, for 2 to 4 weeks afterwards.** At BrainLeaf the support queue took full-time effort for about three weeks plus half to three-quarters time for another week, versus an expected week or two.
6. **There are going to be more problems than you think.** Scaling issues (time zones out of step with buyers, the system grinding down with concurrent users, tired people) — plan extra time and money.
7. **Fix any small problem you know about before the deal.** Small known issues become huge with a thousand new users who do not realise it is small.
8. **Have an SOP for refunds.** You will get refund requests; whether or not the marketplace requires it, at least a few team members (recommended: those who man in-app chats) must know how to issue them.
9. **Don't wait till the last minute to test your ticketing system.** Have an email ticketing system in place first, or requests get confusing, get missed or dropped, and unhappy customers and refund requests follow.
10. **Plan the upsell!** Most groups sell the basic package in the LTD and have the pro plan or upsells ready — make the basic package worth the money (most people should be happy with it) but put clearly wanted/needed features in the pro plan. The author believes he "could have doubled my revenue by doing this."
11. **Having a chatbot ready is critical.** Expect lots of questions; most users expect chatbots now, so it does not drive people away. Plan it out, set it up well, and test with existing users what they use the system for — iterate on chatbot usage before the LTD; it saves countless hours and is worth the upfront spend.
12. **Support Team.** The LTD week brings a huge influx: have **more than one support person**. BrainLeaf had **3 support people and it was not enough** — right away each had **over 20 chats running at the same time**; it became overwhelming very fast. A better chatbot up front might have helped, but one or two people would still have been a mistake.

## Affiliates & Partnerships

**Core principle.** The chapter opens with a quote attributed to Nathan Barry, CEO of ConvertKit: **"Affiliates won't do anything without being pushed."** Put yourself in an affiliate's shoes: they already have people asking them to sell things, an existing revenue stream and little time, so the answer is usually "Sure, give me an affiliate link and maybe I'll mention it. If it sells, then we can push it some more." Lesson 5 of `saas-traction-lessons.md` repeats this: affiliates do basically nothing unless incentivised heavily, so lay it out so easy that no one can mess it up.

**The trick: it's all about the webinar.** Make an offer to the potential affiliate partner that includes a webinar they have to drive their audience toward. At the webinar you make your special offer and give everyone the link to the deal. **The deal should only stay live for a week or two at most**, then the affiliate must run an additional webinar to keep getting more people — this keeps them actively pushing people toward your webinars.

**Deal structures.** Deals can range from **20% of the first payment up to 50% or more of the lifetime value (LTV)** of the customer. Because most SaaS businesses have very high margins, giving away 50% of LTV is often fine to open a new market or access customers you would not otherwise reach. In the deal, remember you are also giving the affiliate's audience some kind of deal — account for how much you are discounting to their users so it does not cut too far into your margins.

**The webinar sequence (5 steps):**

1. **Drop the carrot at the beginning.** Open with something like "This is going to be a 1-hour webinar, and at the end I am going to give you the link to our special offer as well as the free [something else]" — this keeps people listening to the end.
2. **Do the pitch.** You are teaching as well as promoting: give users knowledge they would not otherwise have and show how it works with your system. A SaaS fills a knowledge gap or an efficiency gap — know which yours is and how much time and money it saves.
3. **Make the offer.** Before the Q&A, make the offer they cannot refuse — it must account for the host's cut plus an incentive for the users (a price cut, an additional value metric, or something else).
4. **Give the time limit.** Tell everyone the deal has a time limit, and show them — otherwise people get upset when it expires.
5. **Repeat.** With a good product, the right affiliate, price, message and listeners you probably made sales — so start over and do it again.

## Growth Readiness

`getting-ready-for-growth.md` covers what to put in place once you have users, revenue and feedback. Which path you take depends on the problem you solve and your target market.

**Onboarding.** Once you have a few users you realise the way people start needs improving — development work, additional education, or both. Optimising onboarding can be the difference between people using your system and not; other than the system working, it is one of the biggest adjustable areas of retention. Onboarding should teach what the system does, get the necessary information, and let users implement the SaaS into their systems as quickly as possible. Much of onboarding is marketing automation.

**Letting people think they have a choice.** A recommended trick that "really works well": before or during onboarding, offer people either to watch a video on the system or to sign up for a personalised demo before they can start. In reality everyone always chooses the video, because setting up a meeting takes longer and nobody wants to wait just to look around. People then feel they are choosing to watch a 2 or 3 minute video instead of being forced — which increases understanding dramatically (and retention) while making people feel good.

**Make it simple (but not too simple).** The author has changed onboarding systems many times, usually because the first version was good but too complicated. It is a balance: more often than not SaaS systems start with too much information and have to cut back after the first version.

**'How to use the system' onboarding emails.** Prepare a series of onboarding emails that go out when a new user signs up — every day for a few days in a row, clearly stating that only a few will be sent, and explaining a different system/use each day. These keep users engaged during the critical first few days of considering a new system; use scenarios for use and stories of how the system grew a company or accomplished a goal to connect.

**User tagging for better tracking and action.** When a user signs up they are tagged one way; when they start a project, add a friend, add a teammate, implement a system or use a feature they can be tagged as such and given different information based on what they have done and how. Because different uses imply how likely a user is to continue, tagging during onboarding enables follow-up via email campaigns, direct campaigns, chatbots and even phone calls. (This is covered in more depth in the lead-scoring chapter; see the tagging values there.)

**Keep users involved with ongoing emails and outreach.** Keep people informed, keep giving good information, and make sure they know the lights are on and somebody is there. The position responsible for this ranges "from an 8th of someone's time up to easily a full time position or multiple full time positions" — it is a lot of work.

**The upsell.** Getting ready for a sale means having something to sell; before you start onboarding people you should have an upsell waiting — it can be the difference between getting by and thriving. Examples: the next level up in the system, addons, or a done-for-you approach. For many SaaS systems the upsell is critical because it offsets churn — upgraders upgrade at a higher rate than churners churn.

**Related readiness items from the other files.** The pre-growth foundation checklist in `acquisition-getting-saas-users.md` lists ten things you should have before growing: an understanding of what you are getting into; a validated idea; a specified market you know how to reach; a basically working SaaS; a few key users giving feedback and paying; an onboarding system that is easy and intuitive; emails ready for new users explaining how to use the system; a tagging system (or the start of one) and triggers that show who is doing what and at what level; some users' initial requests for changes and upgrades; and a system that is starting to come together. `narrowing-your-message-with-adaptive-design.md` adds persona readiness: adaptive design presents information based on personas, aspects of a persona and where they are in the buying cycle — education about the problem and its symptoms for someone unaware, and what the system does for that persona for someone in consideration. The author cites Brennan Dunn of RightMessage.com presenting an increase in conversion rate **from 5% to 23%** from a strong adaptive design implementation, and stresses that if you do not know very specifically who you are selling to, you cannot supply the right message — you have to pick your top market rather than design for everyone. Tools named: Leadpages.net (custom landing pages, test messaging), Unless.com and Rightmessage.com (show different data based on conditions, from local weather to market segments, so a homepage looks different by who, what and where).

`saas-traction-lessons.md` supplies further readiness/traction lessons: in the beginning you have to do things that do not scale to enable things that do (the author flew to South Carolina for a potential partner — two days and ~$500 — because of the market access it could unlock); know your competitors inside and out (subscription/demo research; the Sun Tzu quote from *The Art of War*); SEO is critical in SaaS (every SaaS CEO the author knows who does not sell via trade shows and personal relationships uses SEO; lack of SEO understanding is the start of wasted money; recommended reading: Moz's Learn SEO and the SEMrush Digital Marketing Pro academy; pick your keywords up front); community building is key to insight; affiliates need incentives; sales is not marketing and marketing is not sales (marketing gets someone to sign up, sales gets them to pay — "get to know your people first hand, and SELL them"); and making people put in a credit card up front gets better customers in the beginning (people unwilling to risk a card without even a charge are not the users you want when launching — with a nod to freemium sometimes being right).

## Common Mistakes

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Sending outbound emails to a thousand strangers at once | Send ten or twenty, highly researched and personalised; if you cannot research the list, do not send |
| Using your main domain for outbound | Use a close variant (e.g. getyourdomain.com, yourdomain.net) so spam reports do not hit the main domain |
| Skipping DKIM/SPF on the sending domain | Authenticate the sending domain or risk being blacklisted |
| Adding links or images/files in the first two outbound emails | Put the full URL as plain text; let their client make the link; no files early on |
| Sending email from a brand-new Gmail/Gsuite address immediately | Pay for Gsuite ($5/mo), send a few personal emails, and let it sit a full 30 days before business use |
| Starting PPC without keyword research, then scaling | Research keywords, test small campaigns, cut keywords/messaging/demographics that do not convert, then scale |
| Ignoring the exclude-demographics settings online | Spend time in exclude demographics for each item or waste the budget |
| Sending paid traffic to non-converting landing pages | Match pages to keywords and optimise them for conversion |
| Assuming organic conversion rate equals paid conversion rate | Treat them as different rates and measure both; fix content quality and awareness |
| Running one Facebook ad and calling it social media marketing | Use every channel together and reinforce with social media; focus on awareness and funnel entry |
| Selling hard directly from cold social ads | Route cold traffic to the website or a PDF ebook, then upsell to a webinar registration |
| Hiring the cheapest writer ($4.75/hr, $20 for 1,500 words) | Invest in content worth reading; even a good writer needs training on your goals and branding |
| Writing to a word count | Ignore word count; deliver actionable information that solves the reader's problem |
| Posting 3 articles a month then taking a 6-month hiatus | Commit to a schedule you can realistically keep |
| Publishing articles before any keyword research | Do keyword research first — about a day of work — before writing a single article |
| Chasing keywords with difficulty above 20 while domain authority is below 20 | Target low-difficulty keywords first (ideally under 20) and build domain authority |
| Treating an LTD as funding / rushing a product to an LTD | Only do an LTD when the product is ready; many marketplaces refund no-questions-asked |
| Launching an LTD with known small bugs | Fix small problems before the deal — at scale they become business-crushing |
| Staffing an LTD with one support person | Have more than one; at BrainLeaf even three was not enough (20+ chats each) |
| Starting an LTD without a tested email ticketing system or refund SOP | Set up ticketing and a refund SOP, with several team members trained on it |
| Skipping the upsell when running an LTD or onboarding | Have the pro plan/upsell ready — the author believes it could have doubled his revenue |
| Asking an affiliate to "just add a link" | Run a webinar-driven push with a time-limited deal so the affiliate must keep promoting |
| Giving affiliates unlimited-time deals | Keep the deal live only a week or two, then require another webinar |
| Building a full, complex lead scoring formula on day one | Add only the top indicators and actions first; add more as data accumulates |
| Thinking marketing automation is email marketing, set-and-forget, or a magic bullet | It is none of those; it is iterative hypothesise-test-retest work |
| Sending everyone the same messaging regardless of journey stage | Identify who they are and where they are (unawareness → advocacy) before sending |
| Skipping or half-assing SEO/content marketing | Take the time to do it right; almost no successful SaaS owner the author met skipped it |
| Starting complicated onboarding with too much information | Keep it a balance — most SaaS start with too much and must cut it back |
| Letting prospects feel forced into watching the onboarding video | Offer a choice (video vs personalised demo) so they choose the video themselves |
| Building for everyone / not knowing your personas | Pick your top market and build adaptive, persona-matched pages |
| Starting growth without the foundation checklist | Have validation, a specified reachable market, a working system, paying users, onboarding, onboarding emails, tagging/triggers and first feature requests first |
