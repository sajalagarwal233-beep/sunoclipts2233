## Core Frameworks

### What a SaaS is (the author's definition)
A Software as a Service (SaaS) is a cloud-based or partially cloud-based computer program that is constantly being added to, improved, and maintained, and that users pay for on an ongoing basis. Payments are generally monthly or yearly but can be made in a variety of ways.
- These systems almost always require additional business systems: maintenance, customer service, a knowledge base, ongoing development resources, and more.
- SaaS systems often require a high initial investment and often have a high profit margin.
- Commonly cited SaaS systems: Salesforce, Quickbooks, Asana, Zoom, Strava, Spotify, G Suite, Hootsuite, JIRA, Slack.

### The two needs a SaaS fulfills
A SaaS system fulfills one of two needs (every SaaS system does one or both):
1. A **knowledge gap** — it gives the target market knowledge they did not otherwise have.
2. An **efficiency gap** — it makes the target market's job easier by doing something they would otherwise spend time or money on.

### SaaS is a delivery method, not a business model
- SaaS is just a way of delivering software products (and sometimes services) via the internet with no need to install a system on a computer; this is often paired with subscription payment models.
- It enables you to charge in a way that lets you constantly maintain your team and grow the product, and at a certain threshold to have a higher-than-average profit margin.
- Different SaaS businesses have very different clients, outreach methods, customer feedback methods, products, services, and types of employees.
- The author's instruction: do not compare your system to other "SaaS systems" that may have nothing in common with you. Technically Facebook and Twitter could be considered SaaS businesses, and comparing most companies to them is a big ask.
- Cited quote from Waikit Lau (founder of RemoteHQ): "Techcrunch is Hollywood and Facebook is a one in a billion. Having a business like that is the equivalent to winning the lottery."
- The author's framing: even if you are truly great at what you do, you will almost certainly not become a giant; you are just like the other 7.53 billion of us in the world. Expect to work very hard for a long time and fail at least a few times before some success.

### SaaS Success Questions
The author's pre-launch/pre-growth checklist (answer these before launching or growing a SaaS):
- Do you know who you're selling to?
- Are you targeting the right people?
- Do you really know who you're selling to (personas)?
- Do you know what they want and where you provide the most value?
- Have you researched what your competitors are offering?
- Do you know if people will buy your SaaS?
- What are you selling and do you have the right value metric?
- Is your SaaS priced properly? / Do you know how to price your SaaS system?
- Do you know what metrics you should be tracking in your SaaS?
- Do you know how to grow your SaaS revenue?
- Did you ask people what is most valuable to them?
- How do you get the SaaS system built? Does it work? How do you know?
- Is it easy to use (UX + functionality)?
- Do you know how your SaaS will scale?
- What systems will be necessary to support your customers, and to nurture leads and keep customers coming in?
- How will you get your SaaS out there to everyone?
- What is your starting point, and where do you go from there?
- Author's note: the majority of these questions are about pricing, sales, revenue, and business.

### The 4 A's of Your SaaS Machine (the growth levers)
The author's core framework, described as "the buttons you push to make your SaaS company grow":
1. **Appraisement** — setting the price of your system. The author calls it by far the most overlooked lever of SaaS business growth; most entrepreneurs and companies talk about acquisition and attrition but spend little to no time on appraisement. (Dictionary definition he uses: "The act of estimating or judging the nature or value of something or someone.")
2. **Acquisition** — the act of acquiring or gaining possession; getting new users. Different SaaS businesses acquire users in very different ways: direct sales (trade shows + follow-up), search engine marketing and ranking for the right keywords, partnerships, affiliate marketing. Goes hand in hand with Appraisement.
3. **Activation** — the action or process of making something active or operative; helping users start using the system and get value from it immediately so they do not walk away (and probably not come back). The flow is from window shopper to returning customer.
4. **Attrition** — a reduction or decrease in numbers, size, or strength; how many people leave. Also known as "churn" in the SaaS world. The converse is "retainment" — how many people stay.

### Unit economics: the trick behind the A's
You need to know exactly two things:
1. How much money you make from the average customer over their lifetime — **Lifetime Value (LTV)**.
2. The cost of acquiring a new customer — **CAC**.

The rule the author cites: LTV has to be higher than CAC, and according to PriceIntelligently.com it always needs to be higher than **3 to 1**, preferably a lot more — i.e., for every $1 spent to acquire a customer you make $3. The same source is cited as saying that if you continually optimize your pricing, you can get this up to **11 to 1**.

### Lesson 1: When you're building a SaaS, you're building a business
The author's core correction: people think they are building software when in fact they are building a business. He lists 15 upfront and ongoing costs of that business:
1. A federal tax ID (in the US, the first step in starting a new business).
2. Website hosting.
3. A phone number(s).
4. Customer service agents.
5. Customer service software.
6. Electricity.
7. Insurance (may not be needed at first, but will eventually).
8. Security systems (pre-empt hackers rather than pay a ransom, which happens every day).
9. An advertising budget.
10. Marketing.
11. Marketing software.
12. Customer Relationship Management (CRM) software.
13. Sales team members.
14. Money management software.
15. Taxes.

Framing: starting a business is time consuming, has an element of risk, and costs a lot. The author's line on founder hours: "as a new entrepreneur you get to choose your own hours, and that means you can work any 16 hours per day you want."

### Lesson 2: The number one indicator of success — and you're probably going to fail
- The number one indicator of success in SaaS, or any business, is **how many times the person leading the startup has done it in the past**.
- Consequence: you're probably going to fail your first time, so start with something small so you can afford to fail. The author calls this "seriously one of the most important ideas in this entire book."
- "Failure is how we learn to succeed. If you haven't failed, then you probably haven't tried."
- Storytime With Steven (a friend, a successful CEO): his trajectory over four SaaS attempts — 1st crashed and burned, he lost all his money and went back to work as a developer; 2nd made a little money and sold after about a year, still probably lost money counting his time; 3rd at about year 4 or 5, turned it around fast and made a good profit — "a few hundred thousand dollars for each of us"; 4th: a few months after launch, five thousand paying users and cash flowing at over 100k per month, on track to sell for millions.
- Steven's takeaway: "It just takes time and persistence, learning what not to do, and being okay with failure… Being a startup CEO is a profession, and there's no class in the world that will teach you how to do it."

### Lesson 3: High risk, high reward — and the biggest secret to validation
- The most beautiful thing about the SaaS business model is the profit margin; the second is that many SaaS businesses don't need many smart people doing hard jobs that cost a lot of money. You'll need a few developers and designers or an agency to start, but afterwards you can set people to do the same jobs over and over, track their success easily, and hold them accountable — and people who do the same things over and over are generally less expensive or can eventually be replaced by a machine.
- With the reward comes risk: typically a longer time to get to market and a greater potential for failure. Mitigating risk, validating ideas, and decreasing time to market are key contributors to success.
- "Big secret number two": you'll know you have a winning idea when the people you tell keep coming back over and over asking to buy it. If you can get someone to pay you money before you finish or even start building it, you have a winner. If not, either you're not conveying what it does well enough, or what you're building isn't solving a problem worth solving.
- Evidence the author points to: Kickstarter — people buy things before they're started.

### Lesson 4: Platform vs. Tool
- A **tool** is a system that does basically one thing really well. Examples given: Buffer.com, Sumo.com, Calendly.com, Getpocket.com.
- A **platform** is a system that is fully featured and that users work in regularly. Examples given: Asana, JIRA, GSuite, Adobe Creative Cloud.
- "Really, there is a spectrum." The rule: when building a SaaS, especially your first, build a tool — don't make it do everything, just do one thing and do it really, really well.

### Lesson 5: A SaaS business is a sales and marketing company
- "Don't like sales? Don't start a SaaS. Don't like marketing? Don't start a SaaS. Think you're just building a system then people will use it? You're wrong."
- Before the SaaS starts, while it is being built, and especially after the MVP and from there forward, you are running a sales and marketing company. Every aspect of what the business does will be customer-focused and thus sales- and marketing-focused.
- "A SaaS business is a sales and marketing company with a technology component."

### Lesson 6: You have to be able to reach your market
- The first step in reaching a market is choosing a market. You need as specific a target market as possible in mind and you must know how to reach them.
- If you don't know how to get in front of your target market before your product is built, it won't be any easier afterwards. The more specific the group you target, the more likely you are to be able to sell to them.
- Targeting can be specific to: business type, specialization, region, personality, preference, habit, age, gender, income.
- Key: pick something so specific that you would know exactly how to reach a large number of these people immediately and know they have a need for your solution.

### Lesson 7: Little problems become big problems fast at scale
- Narrative example: "Chris," one of the author's first BrainLeaf paying customers, calls to report he can't add new cards on the editor page. The author confirms the bug is reproducible, finds it came from a deployment about an hour ago, then notifies customer service, development, individual developers, and project management; the lead developer responds that they'll have either a rollback or a fix in 10–20 minutes.
- Why the urgency: at scale it isn't one person having an issue, it's about to be a thousand people not being able to use the system — overloading customer service, pulling in project managers, sending developers on wild goose chases, churning customers, losing money and time, setting goals back.
- "When you're working at scale on a system, a tiny little issue can blow up fast." Cross-referenced to the Lifetime Deal chapter, where a few small issues can cause weeks of work and headaches.

### Lesson 8: The Easiest Way To Fail
- Case 1: someone built an "amazing system," spent about **$430k**, then realized he had no idea how to make it successful and offered the whole thing in exchange for slowly paying back his investors.
- Case 2: a lawyer-turned-internet-entrepreneur raised something like **$3 million** to build a music app, received patents, and put together a great team with the goal of selling to big companies — he almost did, but couldn't get it sold and it fell apart.
- The lesson: validate your idea before you spend real money on it. It doesn't matter how good you think your idea is — if you aren't able to get enough customers to put enough money into your pocket to run the business and make money, you are wasting your time.
- Benefits of validating: you know where your money is going, know what you should and should not spend on, work towards bringing in revenue early, and know where you need to cut things off.

### The Process: standard SaaS process steps
The author's path, "From Ideation to Growth," described as intertwining almost all aspects of business building, software development, customer acquisition and activation, and minimizing attrition.

**Phase 1: Business Validation & Presales**
- Validating your business is "**THE MOST IMPORTANT THING**" you can do. Without validation, the chance of success is not one in ten — it is more like **one in one thousand or less**.
- Questions to ask: Why are you doing this? Do you know the market? Do you understand the competition? What will differentiate your business? Do you have existing distribution channels — if not, do you know how to get them? What kind of sale is this (marketing-based, direct, or a mix)? Do you have quantified personas for your buyers (segmentation, how much each is worth, messaging per segment)? Have you surveyed your buyers to confirm they understand and are willing to purchase at your price? What is the cost to build and operate? What is the cost to market and sell? Is your full pricing model accepted? Does your accounting model make sense (can you grow fast enough and well enough to make this work)?

**Phase 2: MVP, Community, SEO, The Hook & List**
- Start marketing *before* building. Five reasons: (1) it takes a lot of time for a website to rank, so get the site and content live as soon as possible; (2) you need to know what people respond to best, which takes serious time; (3) communities don't spring up on their own — someone has to build them and collect the people; (4) you can't email raving fans without emails, and you can't just buy them — you have to gain trust, which takes time; (5) your first idea won't be perfect, so prepare to talk to a ton of people to figure out the best ways to do things.
- Ordered steps: (1) Launch your website; (2) Begin SEO; (3) Find the hook; (4) Start community building; (5) Start gathering emails; (6) Build your system & beta testing; (7) Iterate & ideate on community suggestions.

**Phase 3: Release, Advertise, Marketing, Support, & Grow**
- (1) Release your SaaS (may want to consider a Lifetime Deal); (2) PR + Advertising; (3) Grow your SaaS — podcasts (sponsoring and getting on the circuit), education, blog/SEO; (4) Comparison sites; (5) Channel Partners — affiliate marketing, influencers; (6) Outbound marketing; (7) Support your customers; (8) Grow your business and make money.

**Phase 4: Scale & Grow**
- (1) Grow user base; (2) Increase team size; (3) Grow market share; (4) Scale your company.
- Caveat: every business is different and things change a lot depending on your system and customers — but this is the process the author has seen more often than anything else, and most steps get followed one way or another anyway.

### Parts of a SaaS (anatomy)
The author's list of major areas of a SaaS system — "aspects of most SaaS systems, although not all of them":
1. **Public facing sales & marketing website** — one of your primary sales and marketing tools; must explain the value of the system, what it does for the group, how it works with the target group's existing processes, how to get started (leading to pricing and onboarding), any necessary support systems, pricing, a blog if content marketing is necessary, and a knowledge-base/support area. Typical architecture: Home, Features or Tour, Pricing, Blog, Knowledge base.
2. **User Account Management** — log in/log out, account creation, account deletion and/or suspension, editing user info (name, email, phone), subscription type management, team management (invite team members), permissions management.
3. **User Onboarding** — takes new users through basic setup, educates them on how the system works, instills certainty that the system meets their needs, and gathers information necessary for use. A key portion of both acquisition and retention; varies drastically by system.
4. **User Payment Management Area** — the user-facing system for managing payments, from a simple list of invoices to seat increases and editions. Needs transactional emails for: paid a bill (receipt), owe money (card expired, payment failed), and owe more or less than previously (edition changed, team members added/removed, credits purchased).
5. **Portal/Dashboard** — the main page the user sees after logging in; interact with the system, view statistics, navigate to user and team management. Recommendation: use an extensible pre-built framework such as Angular Material, Bootstrap, or Foundation by Zurb — especially for an MVP.
6. **Payments management system** — where transactions, subscriptions, plans & editions, refunds, and other payment features are viewed and processed. Commonly used: BrainTree Payments, Authorize.net, Stripe.
7. **Admin panel** — the author's top-ranked item that new entrepreneurs do not fully consider when planning a SaaS. Common features: user management (add manually, edit, delete, ban, log in as a user to see and edit their account through the GUI), promo codes (add/edit/delete, manage users with promo codes), system maintenance mode, communication with users (system-wide or individual notifications), viewing system use statistics, data entry and management (especially important if the SaaS is data-based or fills a knowledge gap). Protip: the easier it is for your team to enter and manage data, the faster you'll be able to grow.
8. **SaaS Transactional Email Management** — automatically sent because someone in/using the system did something that triggered it (a "transaction"). Examples: welcome to the system, verify your email, you made a payment, someone was added to your team, your account was cancelled.
9. **User Tagging, Triggers, & Lead Scoring** — described as probably the second most forgotten aspect of SaaS systems when an MVP is developed. When a user signs up, add them to your marketing email list; when they take actions, add them to different lists, send different information, and score/mark them for upsells. Much easier to build in from the beginning than to add later.
10. **Third-Party Management, Tracking, & Reporting Tools Integration** — plan on integrating up front, not later. Areas: money/profit and financial goals (profitwell.com, kissmetrics.com, baremetrics.com); people having trouble and leaving (luckyorange.com, hotjar.com); how people find you, searches, conversion rate (analytics.google.com); selling/confusion (luckyorange.com, hotjar.com); sales tracking and selling to users (CRMs — Pipedrive, ZohoCRM, SugarCRM); social mentions and search ranking (SEMrush.com, ahrefs.com, moz.com); email deliverability (mandrill.com, mailgun.com); helping users and upgrading editions and buying subscriptions (intercom.com, chatbot.com, convertfox.com, verloop.com).
11. **API – Application Programming Interface** — enables developers to work directly and securely with aspects of your system; needed if other systems must get information into or out of your system, plus documentation. Most systems start with core functionality and develop the API after phase 1 — unless the success of your application depends on being connected to other systems, in which case you need the API in the first phase of development.
12. **Unit & Regression Testing** — regression testing is a way of building automated tests that tell you if the system is working correctly when you deploy new features. Depending on the type of SaaS and how well validated it is at launch, you may or may not want tests in the initial system. Author's favorite testing system at the time of writing: Katalon (free to use).
13. **Documentation** — "WRITE DOWN WHAT YOU'RE DOING." Write down as much as you possibly can and share it with everyone. Recommendation: Confluence (integrates with JIRA).
14. **Customer Support Systems** — rather than killing yourself and your reputation with plain email, start with a ticket/request system right away. Recommendations: Helpscout, Zendesk. These allow you to scale and add team members to an infrastructure that scales with the business.
15. **Knowledge base** — a question takes a minute for one customer, but at a thousand customers it scales at a one-to-one ratio and takes a thousand minutes; a knowledge base lets users find answers or be directed to answers very quickly. Options: WordPress with a knowledge base theme (lower-end, works, quick — the author has used the HelpGuru theme a number of times); 3rd-party SaaS knowledge base systems (Zendesk, Confluence, Helpscout, Intercom — whose knowledge base works hand in hand with their chatbot).

## Key Terms & Definitions
- **SaaS (Software as a Service)** — a cloud-based or partially cloud-based computer program that is constantly being added to, improved, and maintained, and that users pay for on an ongoing basis; payments generally monthly or yearly.
- **Knowledge gap** — the need a SaaS fills by giving the target market knowledge they didn't otherwise have.
- **Efficiency gap** — the need a SaaS fills by making the target market's job easier, doing something they would otherwise spend time or money on.
- **Appraisement** — the act of estimating or judging the nature or value of something; in SaaS, setting the price of your system. The most overlooked growth lever.
- **Acquisition** — the act of acquiring or gaining possession; getting new users.
- **Activation** — the action or process of making something active or operative; helping users start using the system and get value immediately.
- **Attrition** — a reduction or decrease in numbers, size, or strength; how many people leave. Also called "churn." The converse is "retainment."
- **LTV (Lifetime Value)** — how much money you make from the average customer over their lifetime.
- **CAC (Cost of Acquiring a Customer)** — the cost of acquiring a new customer.
- **MVP (Minimum Viable Product)** — the first version of the system; after it, you are running a sales and marketing company.
- **Tool** — a SaaS that does basically one thing really well (e.g., Buffer, Sumo, Calendly, Getpocket).
- **Platform** — a SaaS that is fully featured and that users work in regularly (e.g., Asana, JIRA, GSuite, Adobe Creative Cloud).
- **Personas** — quantified buyer/segment definitions (how target markets are segmented, how much each is worth, and the messaging for each).
- **Hook** — the element you find and lead with during Phase 2 to make marketing work.
- **List** — the gathered emails (not bought; earned by trust), a Phase 2 goal.
- **Transactional email** — an email automatically sent by the system because someone in/using the system did something that triggered it (a "transaction"); not limited to monetary transactions.
- **Lead scoring** — scoring or marking users from their in-system actions for upsells and targeted information.
- **Regression testing** — building automated tests that tell you if the system is working correctly when you deploy new features.
- **Admin panel** — the system that lets administrators manage data within the system; the "top item" new entrepreneurs fail to fully consider.
- **API (Application Programming Interface)** — what enables developers to work directly and securely with aspects of your system.
- **Churn** — see Attrition.

## Common Mistakes
| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Thinking you are building software | You are building a business — budget for tax ID, hosting, support, insurance, security, ads, marketing, CRM, sales, accounting, taxes. |
| Treating "SaaS" as a business model | SaaS is a delivery method, not a business model; it is often paired with subscription payments. |
| Comparing your system to other "SaaS systems" with nothing in common with you | Judge your system on its own market, client, outreach, and product; even Facebook/Twitter are technically SaaS. |
| Believing you'll become a giant like Facebook | Facebook is a one-in-a-billion; expect to work very hard for a long time and fail a few times first. |
| Starting with an ambitious platform | Build a tool first — one thing, done really, really well. |
| Starting too big to afford failure | Start with something small so you can afford to fail — you're probably going to fail the first time. |
| Thinking a great build will sell itself | A SaaS business is a sales and marketing company with a technology component. Don't start one if you dislike sales or marketing. |
| Waiting until the product is built to start marketing | Start marketing in Phase 2 (before build): website, SEO, hook, community, emails — ranking and trust take time. |
| Spending real money before validating | Validate first; unvalidated success odds are one in one thousand or less ($430k and $3M failure examples). |
| Vague target market | Pick a market so specific you know exactly how to reach them and that they need your solution. |
| Treating a small at-scale bug as minor | At scale a tiny issue can blow up fast — up to a thousand users affected; roll back or fix immediately. |
| Using plain email for customer support | Use a ticket/request system (Helpscout, Zendesk) that scales with the business. |
| Letting support/answers scale one-to-one | Build a knowledge base so users find answers or are directed to them instantly. |
| Not building in tagging, triggers, and lead scoring | It is the second most forgotten aspect of an MVP; build at least a minimal system up front. |
| Skipping the admin panel | The most commonly under-planned area; the easier your team can enter/manage data, the faster you grow. |
| Waiting to integrate third-party tracking/reporting tools | Plan on integrating them up front — as soon as you launch you'll need them. |
| Not writing anything down | Document massively and share it (the author recommends Confluence). |
| Overlooking pricing | Appraisement is the most overlooked growth lever; monitor LTV:CAC and target above 3:1. |
| Building a custom payments system on day one | Start with Stripe/BrainTree/Authorize.net; go custom only after a certain credit-card revenue point. |

   Defines SaaS and the two needs it fills (knowledge gap, efficiency gap), argues SaaS is a delivery method rather than a business model, punctures startup mythology with the Waikit Lau quote, lists the pre-launch SaaS Success Questions, introduces the 4 A's (Appraisement, Acquisition, Activation, Attrition), and gives the LTV-vs-CAC rule (>3:1, up to 11:1).

2. **Basic Lessons of Saas** — `book/the-business-of-saas/basic-lessons-of-saas.md`
   Eight numbered lessons: SaaS is a business not just software (with 15 business cost items); the #1 success indicator is how many times the leader has done it before, so expect failure (Storytime With Steven's four-SaaS trajectory); high risk/high reward and the "will they pay before you build it" validation secret; platform vs tool; SaaS as a sales and marketing company; reachability of your market; small problems becoming big problems at scale (the Chris/BrainLeaf bug); and the easiest way to fail ($430k and $3M examples).

3. **The Process** — `book/the-business-of-saas/the-process.md`
   A short, structured chapter (framed "From Ideation to Growth") giving the standard four-phase process with concrete checklists: Phase 1 Business Validation & Presales (the validation questions, plus the one-in-one-thousand success warning), Phase 2 MVP/Community/SEO/Hook/List (and five reasons to market before building), Phase 3 Release/Advertise/Support/Grow, and Phase 4 Scale & Grow.

4. **Parts of a SaaS** — `book/the-business-of-saas/parts-of-a-saas-1.md`
   A detailed, example-heavy anatomy of a SaaS system covering 17 component areas — marketing website, user account management, onboarding, user payment management, portal/dashboard, payments management, admin panel, transactional email, tagging/triggers/lead scoring, third-party integrations, API, unit & regression testing, documentation, customer support, and knowledge base — each with the author's concrete tool recommendations and two "protips."

5. **Who & How** — `book/who-and-how.md`
   Short framing chapter: states who the book is for (people with some business experience venturing into SaaS — entrepreneurs, team members, developers, designers, project managers) and why it was written (so many people lost money not understanding what they were getting into). Explains the book is theory, practice, and implementation, and that it is organized around the 4 A's of a SaaS business.

6. **SUMMARY** — `book/SUMMARY.md`
   The book's full table of contents — not a content chapter. It lists every section and chapter used here to map what "The business of SaaS" section does and does not cover, showing that validation, build, pricing, acquisition, activation, and attrition each have their own dedicated later sections.
