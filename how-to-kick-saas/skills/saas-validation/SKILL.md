# SaaS Idea Validation

## When to Use

Use this skill when the user wants to:

- Validate a SaaS idea before spending money on a build ("should I build this?").
- Prove there is demand, a need, and a buyer before writing code.
- Run customer discovery, buyer research, surveys, or advisory-board calls.
- Analyze competitors, competitor features, or competitor pricing, or find a market hole.
- Decide whether an idea is worth doing at all, or size how much validation effort is enough.
- Avoid the failure pattern the author names: build first, then discover nobody wants it.

The author's framing: this is "the MOST IMPORTANT thing in the book, don't skip it," and skipping it is "the thing that drives most SaaS businesses out of business."

## When NOT to Use

- Exceptions are narrow: "Sometimes this process doesn't apply — But most of the time it does." The two named exceptions are that you are already in the market fulfilling requested needs of existing customers, or that you have inside knowledge about the market. Even then, "you will still almost definitely want to go through some aspects of it."
- Not for a builder who only wants to ship the code. The author's advice to a contractor is the opposite of skipping: "Without taking your client through the validation process thoroughly, you are going to get blamed for the failure of the product."
- Not for pricing mechanics or acquisition channels — this skill covers validating the idea itself.

## The Validation Process

The book defines validation as research and states it is specific to SaaS products, that it "works, but only if you do it," and that it always arrives eventually — "upfront planning, mid-build pain, or go to market failure."

### Named aspects of SaaS validation (the book's order)

1. Why & What
2. The problem, Need, Solution
3. Buyers
4. Competitors
5. Distribution
6. Revenue & Costs
7. Time & Investment
8. The secret sauce
9. Going to Market

### The Advisory Approach: the named steps, in order

1. **Write the one-sentence scope:** "My SaaS solves [STATE PROBLEM HERE] for [STATE AS SPECIFIC OF A GROUP AS POSSIBLE HERE]." The easier it is to answer, the easier the sale; the broader the statement and group, the bigger the budget you need to build and market.
2. **Build an advisory group** from your personal network — people in the industry or in your target market. They give feedback, are your first customers, give testimonials, tell friends, and tell you whether the idea is any good.
3. **List competitors and sign up for their systems.** Pretend to be a buyer, get demos, learn how they pitch, record the call, and ask about scenarios, client types, and why they charge what they charge.
4. **Figure out how you are different** on a spreadsheet (one row per competitor product, one column per feature).
5. **Set your pricing model and determine if the business can make money** — two halves: what the market will accept, and whether you profit.
6. **Hire a designer for basic designs** so advisers and potential customers can be walked through the system.
7. **Put the advisory group in a room (physical or virtual)**, ask whether your solution solves their problem and whether the cost is worth it — and if yes, ask them to buy during the meeting. "The rubber meets the road where people open up their pocketbooks and give you money."
8. **Double check with more people.** If the only buyers are people in that room, "you're probably still not going to make it." Ask as many people in the target market as possible.
9. **Validate your distribution channels.**
10. **Score the Validation Success checklist.**

## Core Frameworks

- **Validation is research.** "It is a lot of phone calls, list-making, emails, meetings, surveys, asking questions, changing plans, and sometimes frustration." Without it you are "walking into a smoky casino" and spinning the roulette.
- **The Unique Selling Proposition (USP).** The thing your system has that no one else has, and one of the primary reasons buyers choose you. A USP is less necessary only when the market is fairly untapped, buyers won't research competitors, or competition "can't handle the full load of the market." "Most of the time, especially in SaaS, you have a ton of competitors so you'd better have a great USP!"
- **Is it worth it?** Every feature you discover you must match is "another month or two of work, tens of thousands of dollars, more knowledge base articles to write, more support." "Don't be afraid to admit that it may not be worth it, that's what validation is for."
- **The Product Twist.** Not a pivot: finding a way to do exactly what competitors do, but better or in a new way. It only works after you truly look at how all competitors do everything — and once you twist, "they're going to follow suit."
- **The Market Hole.** Once you know all competitors, their tools, and their target markets, you can find the segment nobody serves. "If it's a market segment that is never going to be served then you're in great shape."
- **Problem + Solution + Need.** All three are required. The common failure is a problem and solution with no need — buyers already use Excel, Word, Google Sheets, or Salesforce and won't move. Your system must be worth their extra time AND their extra money.
- **Audience before tool.** "In so many SaaS businesses what people are missing is an audience, not a tool... you often build an audience, then the tool will sell."
- **Specificity wins.** Hesitating to narrow the market because it feels like lost business is backwards: "specifying your market enables you to sell a lot more."
- **"Don't fall in love with your businesses."** Attributed to the author's grandfather — founders live and die by the idea, "usually more of the latter."
- **Never compete head-on with monsters.** If you cannot explain how you differ from Facebook or Google, you are competing with them. Facebook made about $40 billion in 2017, "about 3.3 billion dollars per month — do you have that kind of budget?"
- **The secret sauce** is assembled from hard-to-find ingredients; the named ones are pre-sales (do you have them, can you get them), the USP, and the unfair advantage.
- **Validation Success checklist.** Eleven statements scored 1 for Yes / 0 for No: "If your score is less than 11, then you're not done validating your system and are in danger of losing all your money and wasting all of your time." Grounded in Sun Tzu: "Every battle is won before it is fought."

## Buyer / Customer Analysis

The book says to research people with the problem, not the product. The named buyer questions are:

- Did you ask people with the problem you are solving? What did they say?
- What is the current market size? Is it worth selling to them?
- Do they have the problem? Do they have the need? Do they know they have a need? If not, why?
- Do they want the product? How much are they willing to pay?
- "If you can't create a list at the beginning of the project, how will you do it later?"
- "Can you make a list of buyers to call today? Most of the time, the answer is no."
- If yes, can you break them apart into the different buyer types?

**How to build the list.** Ask current distributors, then build from LinkedIn (Li), Twitter (Tw), Facebook groups, ads, social ads, forums, podcasts, and tradeshows. Named distributor types: influencers, competitors, podcasts, YouTube channels, Facebook groups, consultants.

**Survey your buyers.** "Get a list of 10 – 1000 people from either your distributors or your lists," and ask who they are (role, how long in business, do they have the problem), whether they have the need, whether the product is wanted, and how much they are willing to pay.

**When you cannot identify buyers — the "fake it up" process.** Named steps: a fake business, a fake website, fake ads to drive ideas, figure out who they are (a Facebook customer validation process), and test your messaging to see if they will buy and to get their contact info.

**Then call them.** "Get on the phone and CALL THEM." Ask who they are, what they want, and how you can help; understand their issues. "You have to do things that don't scale to enable things that do." "You will learn things you never realized about your product and offering."

**Buyer categorization by sales method (the book's explicit split).**

- **Direct sale** — also called an "Enterprise" sale, whether or not the buyer is an enterprise. Sold to a relatively small audience by reaching directly out to someone in the business. Sold at a much higher cost with a longer sales cycle, so there is a high cost of acquisition but a high lifetime value. In the words of enterprise salespeople he has met, "You have to have boots on the ground."
- **Self-serve sale** — built for a larger audience who find you via internet search or advertisement; Slack is the example. "Just the option to use something, especially for free, is a big pull from a paid system." Self-serve users "are going to spend somewhere between $10 and $1000 per month."
- Accessibility, time availability, interest, and sales channels differ, so different validation methods apply; doing BOTH is best when feasible.
- The Competitors worksheet also asks for **buyer types 1, 2, and 3** per competitor (general demographic information), and the features comparison tracks a **target buyer per plan**.

## Competition Analysis Method

Premise: "If you think you're the first person to come up with this idea, you're probably wrong. In fact, you're almost definitely wrong." Two competitor types are defined:

- **Direct competitors:** doing very close to or the same thing, competing for the same buyers and the same dollars.
- **Indirect competitors:** companies or tools that do something similar or accomplish the same tasks (Slack's indirect competitors are named as Google Hangouts and Skype). "Just the option to use something, especially for free, is a big pull from a paid system."

Steps, in the author's numbered order (the source skips from 2 to 4):

1. **Search Google**, broad then refined. Expect useless, SEO-driven results at first; his own search returned mostly ads and a definition.
2. **Social media requests** — start here because replies take days. Ask Facebook (his examples: the SaaS Mantra group and the SaaS Growth Hacks group) and ask LinkedIn, which he notes has become "a lot about viral videos and a lot less about real questions and answers."
4. **Search ProductHunt.com**, then, if needed, post on the makers discussion board asking for niche keywords you don't yet know.
5. **Search review and comparison sites:** G2, Owler, and SimilarWeb.
6. **Check with SEO tools.** His favorite is Ahrefs.com — "Sign up for the $7 trial, enter one competitor and search for their competing websites."

Then:

- **Fill in the Competitors worksheet.** Fields: business or competitor name, URL, pricing page URL, trialed (yes/no), similarity to your product (1–5 scale), competitor type (direct or indirect), value metric, buyer types 1–3 (more as needed), and major features 1–3 (more as needed).
- **Demo competitor systems.** Take the trial if there is a freemium tier; otherwise request a demo. Ask: how long have you been with the company, what is the best feature, where do people struggle and why, biggest customers, the difference from a named competitor, what is on the roadmap, how did the business get started, how did you get your first customers, where do most customers come from, and which feature is most used. "Make sure to record the audio and screens during your recordings!" If you can't record, take screenshots and have someone else take notes.
- **Line up the top three to five competitors** in the features worksheet. For each plan record: value metric, plans per competitor, cost, target buyer per plan, features per plan, and the **highlighted** features on their pricing pages ("generally the features at the top of the page that each user group is most interested in").
- **Then answer the five questions:** Do I have a unique selling proposition? Can I produce a competitive advantage? Is there a hole in the market? Could I twist this product to enter in a new way? Do I need to produce the same features as my competition to be competitive?
- **Warning on price:** "Just about all SaaS businesses have great margins, so if your competitive advantage is price, your competitors who are already in the market can probably change their prices to match yours. Be careful about listing price as a competitive advantage in SaaS!"
- **If you find no competition**, see the Decision Tables. "If no one else is doing this, that there is more than likely a good reason for that." One monopoly example: a congress-mandated order giving Health Grades a monopoly on certain Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services data.
- **Real case:** chasing a lead-generation tool, the author's first Google searches looked empty, then Facebook groups produced a long competitor list, and a contact said the project needed "a 4 or 8 man team working for a year" and might still not be figure-out-able. He stopped the project.

## Time & Money Of Validation

Figures as the author states them:

- **Two years to market penetration.** "From ideation to market penetration, my experience is that most companies come in at around two years." And: "plan on it taking at least a year to get the product right and understand your market fit. Then plan on another year of iterating on the product."
- **Double the build estimate.** "What is the cost to build the system? - choose a round number, then double it."
- **Double the market-share estimate.** "How long will it take to gain market share? - pick an estimated time of at least 1 year and double it."
- **Growth-rate assumptions.** "What is your growth rate - start at 1% for AT LEAST the first 4 months, then you can go up to 2% to 5%. Aim for 20% within 6 months."
- **Monthly cost categories:** people, software, marketing, advertising, operations.
- **Design budget.** "Plan on spending between $1200 and $5000 for a first round of designs." Expect revisions and hire a UX designer who has done this before.
- **Cost of validation itself, by comparison.** "Would you rather spend 2k figuring out if this will work or 100k building something you'll never make a dollar from?" (a tradeshow visit versus an unvalidated build).
- **Case-study magnitudes:** David, a $60k investment and a year of work lost, after previously spending "ten to thirty thousand dollars per year with consultants"; Jake, a $60k budget against Facebook's "$1B+ a year in revenue"; Eyal, "almost half a million dollars" from friends.
- **Compliance multiplies cost.** For MedRev, HIPAA compliance made operations "SIX TIMES more expensive" than sending ordinary business surveys.
- **Sales-method price anchors.** Self-serve: $10–$1000 per month. Enterprise example: "$200,000 per year per business."
- The book gives **no single deterministic validation budget**. Validation cost is framed as the 2k vs 100k comparison and as lost capital in the case studies, not as a quoted figure.

## Decision Tables

**Validation Success checklist** — eleven statements scored 1/0; 11 of 11 is required. The statements: scope sentence completed; all major competitors reviewed (features, how they sell, to whom); a USP; designs that walk advisers or potential customers through the system and are easy to understand; advisers who think it is a good idea and have given you money; a survey where buyers say they will buy and indicate pricing; at least a few users willing to talk about buying before it is built; several distribution methods identified and tested as definitely working; a pricing structure customers accept that makes money; a budget to build and distribute; and the enthusiasm and time to do it.

**Reasons to build a SaaS, and what each implies:**

| Reason | The author's read |
| --- | --- |
| I need it for myself or my company | Not a broad tool but a specific tool around your processes; odds others adopt your process are not good, and you often end up competing with Excel or Slack. Do substantial competitor research first. |
| It's a good idea and people need it | Fine if you already know the industry. If not, "you're going to have to learn an entirely new career" and it will take a long time. |
| I want to make money | If it is the only reason and you lead the business, "I would recommend no doing this and investing in more tested businesses" — real estate or the stock market. |
| I have knowledge others do not | Falls into the "filling a knowledge gap" SaaS; you probably also know people in the industry or have influence. |
| I already have a system built | Happens a lot with developers. You still need full validation, especially of costs: developers, changing integrations, compliance, insurance, support, legal. |
| Similar system exists and I can do it cheaper/better | "One of my favorite reasons" — you have a USP, an unhappy market, and proof people buy. |
| I have a market that needs it | You are "already well on your way to success"; the tool will sell to the audience. |
| I want to start a business | "Probably the worst reason on the list." If you just want a business, "invest in real estate." |
| I have money put away | The number one indicator of SaaS success is how many times the founder has done it before; if you run the business side, "make sure you have a competent CTO already in place." |
| I was hired to build it | Ensure your client validates first, or "you are going to get blamed for the failure of the product." |

**If you can't find any competition:**

| Cause | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| It may not be a viable market | Indirect competitors too strong, market too small or unreachable, or buyers have little money to spend. |
| They already went out of business | The market may already have rejected the model. |
| There is a monopoly | Especially in regulated industries such as healthcare. |
| There is already a solution that does not require a SaaS | Buyers solve it without software. |
| You found something truly new | Possible, but "probably not though." Even then validation is harder because there is nothing to compare against. |

**Direct (enterprise) vs self-serve buyer:**

| Dimension | Direct / Enterprise | Self-serve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience size | Relatively small | Larger |
| How they find you | Direct outreach; "boots on the ground" | Internet search, ads, affiliate marketing |
| Price | Much higher | Roughly $10–$1000 per month to start |
| Sales cycle | Longer | Shorter |
| Economics | High acquisition cost, high lifetime value | Lower acquisition cost |
| Example in the book | Dr. Paul selling to hospitals | Slack |

## Common Mistakes

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Hire a team and build first, then figure out sales | Query target buyers for a few hours to a few days first |
| Build for yourself and assume others adopt your process | Treat it as a specific tool competing with Excel/Slack and do substantial competitor research |
| Believe no one has your idea because the first Google search was thin | Keep searching social, ProductHunt, review sites, and SEO tools until competitors appear |
| Assume no competition is good news | Assume one of the five causes and dig |
| List price as your competitive advantage in SaaS | Expect competitors to match your prices; find a real advantage |
| Chase a broad multi-billion-dollar market | Specify the target market and offering; narrow markets let you sell more |
| Stop at problem + solution without confirming the need | Confirm the need; buyers on Excel/Word/Salesforce won't pay for marginal value |
| Validate only with your advisory board | "If the only people that buy your system are sitting in the room with you, then you're probably still not going to make it" |
| Use the quoted build cost as your plan | Choose a round number, then double it |
| Assume fast early growth | Start at 1% growth for at least the first 4 months, 2–5% after, aim for 20% within 6 months |
| Assume high SaaS margins absorb extra costs | Run the numbers; HIPAA compliance made one product's operations six times more expensive |
| Accept a build request without validating the client's idea | Insist the client validates, or you own the blame for the failure |
| Expect a short timeline to market | Plan around two years from ideation to market penetration |
| Validate only after the money is spent | Do it up front: validation comes as upfront planning, mid-build pain, or go-to-market failure |
