# SaaS Growth Hacks & Author's Notes

## When to Use

Use this skill when the user wants:

- Growth hacks, quick-win tactics, or "no one wants you to know" plays for a SaaS business.
- Pricing help: how to price, value metrics, price sensitivity testing, pricing page design, willingness-to-pay questions, plan-per-persona packaging.
- Upsell, expansion, or monetization ideas ("growth levers").
- Churn reduction, retention, or attrition plays.
- Referral, virality, affiliate, influencer, or channel-partner / partnership plays.
- Acquisition and marketing levers: organic SEO, keyword research, content tricks, Medium, alternative-to pages, webinars, podcasts, LTDs, outbound, PPC, Facebook ads.
- Onboarding and product/UX plays that affect conversion.
- The raw, unstructured notes and outline material the author left behind — the appendices-style material: the pre-launch readiness checklist, the 12-step sequence, the 3 A's, the role-by-role needs list, the favorite resources and recommended reading.

## When NOT to Use

- The user wants the book's full, structured teaching (validation, costing, development, support). This skill only carries the notes chapter and its commentary; point them at the book itself.
- The user wants step-by-step engineering, code, or developer/UX tutorials. The book explicitly does not teach that.
- The user wants a guaranteed, "foolproof" growth process. The author states plainly that this process is "not foolproof" and that many businesses have tried it and failed.
- The user wants current, verified market data. The figures here are the book's own stated figures and its own cited study, unattributed to a live source.
- The user is asking for grey/black-hat tactics to run. The author describes these tactics but explicitly does not recommend them ("I'm not going to recommend you do this since the penalties can be stiff").

## Growth Hacks By Category

### Pricing & Monetization

- **Treat pricing as foundational, not an afterthought.** The section "Pricing is foundational" heads the Appraisement list. The author notes pricing is "often ignored because it touches everything," and that "the average SaaS company spends 6 hours over their whole lifecycle on pricing."
- **Separate the three levers consciously:** "Positioning = people," "Packaging = presentation," "Pricing = pricing." "If you position price package effectively, then a significant part of your sales and marketing job is done already. Without it, you are attracting the wrong people that don't fit your value."
- **Use value-based pricing, not cost-plus or competitor-based.** "Your SaaS company exists to offer value to your customers. This is why the only viable option in SaaS pricing is value based pricing." He adds it "could be called 'customer based pricing'." He still frames cost-plus vs. competitor-based vs. value-based as "your own strategy."
- **Never price identically to incumbents.** "If you are just giving someone what is already out there with the same packaging, same price, etc. they are just going to go with what is already out there."
- **Pick a value metric that scales with the customer.** "You need to have something that scales up cost with the size of the business. Period." A value metric is "what you're selling that determines price," and it must exist "1. To be easy to understand 2. To align with your customer's needs 3. To grow with your customer."
- **Tune the thresholds, then keep testing.** "Growing with your customers is all about getting the thresholds for your value metric correct" — persona willingness-to-pay data gives the starting point, but "the only way to drill down on exactly what works is to continually test and optimize." "Value metrics are the best way to optimize your pricing for growth."
- **Run the Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity meter.** These are the four questions:
  1. "At what price would you consider the product to be so expensive it is not worth buying?" (too expensive)
  2. "At what price would you consider the product starting to get expensive, so that it is not out of the question, but you would have to give some thought to buying it?" (expensive/high)
  3. "At what price would you consider the product to be priced so low that you would feel the quality couldn't be very good?" (too cheap)
  4. "At what price would you consider the product to be a bargain — a great buy for the money?" (cheap/good value)
  His caveat before the questions: "People are not good at tell you if your product is worth $99/mo or $79/mo."
- **Hunt the market trough, then trade volume for LTV.** "The highest share of the market is found in the trough at the bottom. This is the price point where the maximum percentage of sales can be found. However, you can also see other price points that may return higher profits. In this case, moving from $200 to $400 barely sacrifices market but potentially has far better LTV per customer."
- **Consider price localization.** Listed as a requirement when building the pricing page, alongside the warning that "by discounting, you have already hurt that value" and that "Discounting Undervalues Your Product Both Externally and Internally."
- **Build quantified buyer personas; never guess.** "Quantified buyer personas are the foundation of your entire pricing strategy." "Without advanced buyer personas, you are reduced to 'guess and check' marketing and development."
- **Build personas from existing customer data, not brainstorming.** "Look at current customers and build profiles from there. Use profitwell to pull up payment info and sort by MRR." Needed for each persona: company size, industry, role.
- **Give each persona its own plan.** "One plan per persona." Each persona needs three elements: (1) demographic data — size and type of target company plus title and role definitions, "based on current customer data rather than just brainstorming"; (2) valued features — from market research and feature value analysis, with each persona valuing different features; (3) willingness to pay — price sensitivity data showing the ranges each persona will pay.
- **Name personas by one word of demographic:** "a bootstrapper, a startup, a growing company, or a large unicorn." Willingness to pay "grows with the growing demographics and features."
- **Match features to persona taste.** "The Bootstrap persona only wants quick and easy access to the product, while Growth wants custom analytics and support."
- **Make the pricing page "the most important page on your marketing site"** — "It is the one that is going to convince prospects to sign up or not. To part with their hard-earned revenue or not. So the design has to be right."
- **Lead with the feature they value.** "If you know what feature your customers really value then that should be the first thing they see when they hit your landing page. Your sub-landing pages can be designed around each of the main features for each buyer persona." And route the funnel accordingly: "You can construct your sales funnel to pass through different features as potential customers travel to your pricing page."
- **Pricing scenario levers.** To *raise LTV*: "higher prices and better retention." To *lower CAC*: "better positioning and packaging to target ideal customers."

### Upsell & Expansion

- **Upselling and "Affiliates & Other Partnerships"** sit in the chapter outline itself, directly after PPC and Continuous Marketing.
- **Expansion comes free from a correct value metric**, because the metric is chosen "To grow with your customer" and the value metric "means you grow as your customers grow."
- **Sell up by plan tier, not by nagging:** each persona gets one plan, and willingness to pay "grows with the growing demographics and features," so the higher tier is sold on the features the higher persona values (Growth "wants custom analytics and support" versus the Bootstrap persona's "quick and easy access").
- **Treat churn reduction as a pricing asset:** "Reducing churn comes from giving customers true value, which they get if the positioning is precise."

### Churn & Retention

- **Promote the four attrition sections as the program:** Community building, Supporting your customers, Knowledge base, Education, Constant communication, "Keeping up with the Joneses."
- **Work your Lifetime Deal buyers into advocates.** "How to use customers from your LTD to become brand advocates," then "activation is not there you can run promotions referral deals."
- **Segment by activity, don't blast everyone.** "See how often they are using your app — identify who is actively using the tool — who use sometimes — who never take action — people who signed up because it is free."
- **Email the engaged and ask.** "See who is willing to engage with you send emails with surveys." The survey question: "what stops you from using the tool?" And the rule for the rest: "if they are not willing to answer the question then let them go."
- **Book 15-minute calls, multiple ones.** "Ask them for a call — 15m only — make sure you have questions ready — what is stopping them from using the app — what do they want to see from using your app — rapid fire question round." Then: "better to have multiple calls — create multiple engagements with customers."
- **Close the loop in public.** "Once you have the feedback and have implemented it, then let them know it has been added — creates incentive to go back and use the system."
- **Make emails interactive.** "Recommends making emails very interactive. Lots of emails do not get read. Lots of emails get unsubscribed. Start interactive emails with questions. Questions and quizzes are working well. Need to be interactive."
- **Gate live chat.** "Chat support only for paying customers — so that you can spend more time on getting clients onboarded."

## Benchmarks & Numbers

Attributed to the book and its own stated study:

- **The 512-company study:** "This data, taken from our study of 512 SaaS companies, shows that just a 1% improvement in monetization allows you to increase revenue by 12.7%. This makes it by far the best growth lever in your business. Monetization is almost 4X more effective than acquisition at raising your bottom line."
- **Growth levers, as listed:** Acquisition, Retention, Monetization.
- **LTV/CAC:** "LTV/CAC is the math your entire business is built on." "LTV/CAC ratio of >1 is good, but not good enough. You need substantially higher LTV than CAC. At least a 3 to 1 ratio to run a successful business. But more like an 11 to 1 ration [ratio]."
- **Formulas given:** CAC = total cost of sales and marketing / # of customers acquired. "In the CPC channel… CAC = CPC / % trial x % paid." ARPU = Average Revenue Per User. LTV = ARPU / Churn Rate. LTV (simplified) = (sum of all customer mrr / total # of customers) / (# customers who churned / total # of customers). Value metric = what you're selling that determines price.
- **On cutting CAC:** "cutting back on CAC is generally the unfavored option to increase your LTV/CAC ratio. Customers, particularly the high-value ones, cost money to acquire. Cutting back here means cutting back on acquisition, leading to further problems."
- **Time spent on pricing:** "Average SaaS company spends 6 hours over their whole lifecycle on pricing."
- **The price-sensitivity example:** moving "from $200 to $400 barely sacrifices market but potentially has far better LTV per customer" (relative to the market-share trough).
- **The daily-offer example:** a $50/mo SaaS closed at 10% of calls yields "an additional $600/yr" every 10 days.
- **The follower threshold:** under ~20,000 Twitter followers, no one with 100,000+ followers takes you seriously; the mechanic is the follower-to-following ratio.
- **The persona structure:** 1 plan per persona, 3 elements per persona (demographic data, valued features, willingness to pay); 4 example demographics (bootstrapper, startup, growing company, large unicorn).
- **The 4 price-sensitivity questions**, 4 personas, 3 things per persona, 3 growth levers, "The 3 A's," and the 12-step sequence (Idea, Initiate, Validate, Learn, Presell, Create, Lead, Organize, Grow, Learn, Repeat, Thrive).
- **The 20-keyword seed list** for SEO keyword research.
- **The stage framing:** acquisition can take "years sometimes."

## The Author's Raw Notes

Themes and arguments from notes-1.md, arranged so an agent can act on them.

**1. The activation gap after a Lifetime Deal.** His opening note diagnoses the core post-LTD problem: "how to use customers from your ltd to become brand advocates activation is not there." The fix sequence is referral deals, usage-frequency segmentation, survey outreach, calls, and closing the feedback loop — an activation program rather than a discount campaign.

**2. Retention is earned by contact, not by features.** The retention block is short and behavioral: find out what stops people using the app, ask the people who will engage, let the rest go, take the call, make the call short and prepared, and tell people when their feedback shipped. Multiple engagements beat one big survey. Emails must be interactive because plain ones go unread and drive unsubscribes.

**3. Protect your support time and your margins.** "Chat support only for paying customers — so that you can spend more time on getting clients onboarded." Non-paying users consume the resource that should go to onboarding buyers.

**4. Go international by influencing the influencers, and go head-to-head where you speak the language.** For non-English markets, find the niche influencers in those countries; in English-speaking markets, approach the influencers who already promote your competitors.

**5. Growth through teaching.** His central growth argument: "The best way to grow your business is to teach. It also happens to be one of the best things you can do to help humanity." Every article, video, training, seminar, course, worksheet and quiz his reader has consumed "led you to this point" and simultaneously grew the reputation of its author. "If it's worth buying, it's worth learning about, and there are people who want to learn all over the world. So teach it!" Three channels: socially (articles, YouTube), locally (Chamber of Commerce classes, schools, Meetup), individually (mentoring). His ProTip: "At the end of the day, business is about relationships. The more people you know, the more people you have helped, and the more people who like you, the more value you have as a person and the more people want to do business with you."

**6. Ads to jumpstart, SEO to sustain.** If SEO wasn't started early, lead with paid social or AdWords, but hold the long-term line on SEO — rank first and "you DON'T NEED so much advertising."

**7. The three things that always grow a business (learned, not invented).** He credits a seminar by John Logar of Consulting Unleashed, who gave four items — one was "Teach What You Know" (covered earlier in the book) and "the other one doesn't apply to SaaS." The remaining ones he lists are:
- **Understand a new customer once a week** — in person, ideally over lunch, using his script; ask about likes, dislikes, painful features, requests, daily work, processes, and inter-office language.
- **Make an offer every day** — "Every. Single. Day." Same offer or special, you or a hire, but never zero. His arithmetic: $50/mo at a 10% close rate adds $600/yr every 10 days, and "these things snowball" because you get better each time.
- (He also sets "Set an appointment every day" alongside it in the growth list.)
He frames the whole chapter as, again, "Can't tell you how to be successful, but I can tell you how not to fail," with an explicit disclaimer: "This is a good process. It's worked for some businesses, but it's not foolproof, and for every business that has succeeded, there are tons of other businesses that have tried this and failed."

**8. Do things that don't scale so you can scale** — invite-gated signups where you talk to everyone who signs up, refusing to call the launch a beta, and physical presence ("Be in New York"). The observed pattern: "It's NOT a fast thing — years sometimes."

**9. The launch mechanics he insists on.** Get everyone you know on the workable product and harvest their comments for the site; find the "Hook" (his example: Slack as "email killer"); invite people to the preview release; then launch publicly; drive every channel's traffic to one centralized place; use podcasts (run and sponsor), comparison sites, welcome mats with review-collecting CTAs, integrations that piggyback on your customers' existing systems; write on Medium in four content modes; steal competitor traffic with "alternative-to" pages; import content and post controversial articles; use the "starter kit" play (Intercom's) and the Assessment CTA; set page titles to carry the message; apply AIDA; use giveaway freebies.

**10. His frank position on grey-hat growth.** He documents unfollow campaigns and the 20k-follower reality as observed industry practice, states the penalties can be stiff, and explicitly declines to recommend it, while saying he "may know someone who tried this with great success." The legal, sustainable alternative he gives is consistent great content over a long time — "which you should be doing anyway."

**11. The pre-launch readiness checklist.** A long, explicit "before you begin" list of conditions. His framing: validation first and money only after costing. Conditions he lists include: the project is validated and people have pre-purchased; major user groups, desired features and value metric determined; plans and costs figured out; pricing page planned; team selected; tech stack decided; project management tool selected and taught to the team; project plan written and accepted by all team members and stakeholders; information architecture written; documentation system planned; admin panel data-management needs outlined; payment system chosen, accounts set up, bank accounts connected; unit testing system chosen and integration planned; user processes generally planned; transactional emails planned and designed; user flows designed; page designs and views created and agreed upon; integrations generally planned; initial user tagging and triggers planned; third-party systems considered and planned; development environment set up; code repository selected and created; remote deployment prepared; automation server for continuous integration selected and planned; dependency and systems costs planned; third-party systems costs planned; system build labor costs estimated; operational labor costs estimated; initial marketing and advertising costs determined; and all initial costs funded with ongoing costs planned or funded.

**12. The 12-step sequence he offers for the whole journey:** Idea, Initiate, Validate, Learn, Presell, Create, Lead, Organize, Grow, Learn, Repeat, Thrive.

**13. What the reader should be able to do by the end.** Validate an idea and get pre-sales; understand the aspects of a SaaS; build a team; develop the system; price it; release an MVP; acquire customers and keep them from leaving; build a community; support customers; manage the development growth of the system. Then the three questions he says come up at that point: "What is my process? Is there a steps I should take now? How do I gain traction?" — followed by his line: "You can build the greatest thing in the world, but if no one knows about, it doesn't do a whole lot of good."

## Common Mistakes

| Wrong | Correct |
| --- | --- |
| Spend almost no time on pricing — "the average SaaS company spends 6 hours over their whole lifecycle on pricing" | Treat pricing as foundational and revisit it; "the only viable option in SaaS pricing is value based pricing" |
| Match the incumbents on packaging and price | Give a differentiated package and price, or "they are just going to go with what is already out there" |
| Cut CAC to improve the LTV/CAC ratio | Raise prices and improve retention to lift LTV; "cutting back here means cutting back on acquisition" |
| Compete on discounts | Avoid discounting — "by discounting, you have already hurt that value"; discounting "Undervalues Your Product Both Externally and Internally" |
| Use guessed or brainstormed personas | Build quantified buyer personas from current customer data (profitwell, sorted by MRR) — "Without advanced buyer personas, you are reduced to 'guess and check' marketing and development" |
| Ask people whether the product is worth $99/mo or $79/mo | Run the four Van Westendorp price-sensitivity questions |
| Charge a flat fee that never changes with the customer | Have a value metric that "scales up cost with the size of the business" |
| Let the market-share trough dictate your only price point | Compare price points — "$200 to $400 barely sacrifices market but potentially has far better LTV per customer" |
| Blast all users with plain emails | Make emails interactive, "start interactive emails with questions," use surveys and quizzes |
| Chase every disengaged signup | Ask the willing, and "if they are not willing to answer the question then let them go" |
| One big research survey then silence | "better to have multiple calls" — "create multiple engagements with customers" |
| Collect feedback and never mention it again | Tell users when their feedback "has been added" — "creates incentive to go back and use the system" |
| Give live chat to everyone | "Chat support only for paying customers" so onboarding gets the time |
| Launch without an audience | Use launch methods that require one — LTDs, advisory boards, partnerships with audiences and sales groups |
| Call the launch a beta | "Don't call it a beta" |
| Rely on ads alone for the long term | "Jumpstart with advertising, sustain through SEO" |
| Skip SEO until launch | Start SEO before the product is ready — if you didn't, ads must carry you until SEO takes over |
| Sell only to the English-speaking market | Pitch non-English-speaking audiences and find niche influencers there |
| Target unfamiliar influencers blindly | In English-speaking markets, approach influencers who already promote your competitors |
| Run a grey-hat unfollow campaign | Produce great content regularly over tons of time — the author documents the grey-hat play but does not recommend it because "the penalties can be stiff" |
| Let anyone else own the early selling | "Up front, you need to be able to sell your own stuff. No one else is going to be able to grow this initially the way you can" |
| Wait for inbound to make offers | "Make an offer every day. Every. Single. Day." |
| Assume the process guarantees success | Accept the disclaimer — it's "not foolproof"; "for every business that has succeeded, there are tons of other businesses that have tried this and failed" |
